Summary

When we walk, run, cycle, or propel a wheelchair, our muscles act as the motors of the biological system, driving the limbs in repetitive, cyclic motions that generate movement. During each cycle, muscles repeatedly lengthen (eccentric contraction) and shorten (concentric contraction), a pattern known as the stretch–shortening cycle (SSC). Muscles deliver negative mechanical power when producing force while lengthening, whereas they deliver positive mechanical power when producing force while shortening. Across a movement cycle, these phases result in negative and positive mechanical work, the sum of which determines the net mechanical work produced by the muscles over one full cycle. Dividing this net mechanical work by the cycle duration yields the average mechanical power output (AMPO), a key determinant during self-propelled high-speed motor tasks. Yet, despite its importance, surprisingly little was known about how muscles should operate in order to maximise AMPO in a particular movement task and, related, how much AMPO we can maximally generate with our muscles.

Put in its simplest form, the main question addressed in this thesis is: What muscle length over time and corresponding muscle activation over time maximises AMPO? In addition, there are several related questions such as: What is the influence of muscle length excursion and cycle frequency on AMPO? Do these parameters interact? Trying to answer these questions using experiments alone provides limited knowledge as only a small set of all possible SSCs can be investigated within a measurement session: Which SSCs should be selected to gain as much insight as possible? In this thesis, we addressed these questions using experimental techniques in combination with simulation and optimisations of mathematical muscle–tendon complex models in order to gain fundamental insight into the power-producing capabilities of muscles.

In 2  Accuracy of experimentally estimated muscle properties: Evaluation and improvement using a Hill-type model and a newly developed toolbox and 3  Mechanical power output during stretch–shortening cycles of rat medial gastrocnemius muscle: influence of various muscle length trajectories, we focused on the muscle–tendon complex (MTC) of the rat hindlimb. The advantage of working on an isolated MTC preparation was that we had full control over the MTC length and activation over time. Thus, we could readily test the influence of various SSC parameters (e.g. cycle frequency and length excursion) on the maximally attainable AMPO. Nevertheless, as mentioned earlier, only a limited set of SSCs can be tested in a single measurement session. To address this, we complemented experimental work with predictions derived using Hill-type MTC models. Obviously, model predictions are only valuable if they are sufficiently accurate. Our approach was to evaluate model predictions against a set of insightful experimental conditions. If the model predictions proved sufficiently accurate, it would justify to use the model to further explore the SSC parameter space beyond what could be experimentally tested. Importantly, AMPO is influenced by muscle properties, such as maximal isometric force and muscle fibre length as well as all other properties of the contraction and excitation dynamics. Thus, to reduce inter-individual variance in predictions due to differences in MTC properties, we chose to estimate the properties of each individual MTC based on commonly used experimental protocols. This raises an important question: Can these properties be estimated with sufficient accuracy using these protocols?

2  Accuracy of experimentally estimated muscle properties: Evaluation and improvement using a Hill-type model and a newly developed toolbox presents a theoretical study on the accuracy of commonly used experimental protocols for estimating key MTC properties in isolated MTC preparations, such as quick-release and step-ramp experiments. We simulated these protocols with a Hill-type MTC model based on parameter values obtained from literature. We then estimated the parameters from the simulated data and compared them with their a priori values. In quick-release experiments, MTC first contracts isometrically (i.e. at a constant length), after which a quick change in length is imposed (hence the name: quick-release). A common assumption underlying the quick-release experiments is that the release is so fast that muscle fibres do not shorten during the quick-release. Consequently, under this assumption, all length change is taken up by the series elastic element (SEE) in series with the muscle fibres. SEE stiffness can then be estimated from its force and length changes measured before and after the release. However, we found that this assumption is invalid: muscle fibres do shorten even within quick-releases of only 10 ms. Consequently, SEE stiffness is substantially underestimated (by about 35%) under this commonly used assumption. This underestimation in SEE stiffness subsequently propagates to the estimation of other MTC properties, with estimations of excitation dynamics properties being affected most strongly (up to about 20%). We developed an improved method that accounts for the shortening of muscle fibres during quick-releases. This improved method yielded parameter estimates that were close to their a priori values (within 3%). This improved method was then used in 3  Mechanical power output during stretch–shortening cycles of rat medial gastrocnemius muscle: influence of various muscle length trajectories.

In 3  Mechanical power output during stretch–shortening cycles of rat medial gastrocnemius muscle: influence of various muscle length trajectories, we combined in situ experiments with simulations and optimisations using a Hill-type MTC model to investigate the influence of various SSCs on the maximally attainable AMPO of rat m. gastrocnemius medialis. For each individual specimen, key MTC properties were estimated individually using the methodology developed in 2  Accuracy of experimentally estimated muscle properties: Evaluation and improvement using a Hill-type model and a newly developed toolbox. This enabled a direct comparison between experimentally measured and model-predicted AMPO for each specimen. We found near-perfect agreement between experimentally measured and model-predicted AMPO (r² > 0.98), which then justified the use of the Hill-type MTC model to systematically explore how variations SSCs influence the maximally attainable AMPO. We predicted the maximally attainable AMPO for a large set of SSCs, parametrised by cycle frequency, FTS (the fraction of the cycle time shortening; e.g. an FTS of 0.65 means that 65% of the cycle time is spent shortening) and MTC length excursion. AMPO was predicted to peak at a cycle frequency of 3.5 Hz, an FTS of 0.85 and an MTC length excursion of 8 mm. Notably, cycle frequency and MTC length excursion showed a strong interaction: increasing one necessitated a decrease in the other in order to maximise AMPO. By contrast, the optimal FTS remained remarkably constant across all tested combinations of cycle frequency and MTC length excursion. 3  Mechanical power output during stretch–shortening cycles of rat medial gastrocnemius muscle: influence of various muscle length trajectories provides an extensive overview of how SSC parameters influence the maximally attainable AMPO of rat m. gastrocnemius medialis.

Given the success of the approach used in 3  Mechanical power output during stretch–shortening cycles of rat medial gastrocnemius muscle: influence of various muscle length trajectories, we applied a similar approach in 4  What is the influence of knee joint movement on the maximal average mechanical power output of human quadriceps femoris muscle? to investigate the effect of SSC parameters on the maximally attainable AMPO of the human m. quadriceps femoris in vivo. However, the approach differed in several key aspects. First, instead of individually tailored models for each participant, we employed a Hill-type model with generic MTC properties derived from literature to investigate the influence of SSC parameters on the maximally attainable AMPO. Second, in the experimental setup, knee joint movements were fully imposed using a knee dynamometer, while participants were instructed to maximise AMPO. Thus, muscle activation was not directly controlled but resulted from voluntary neural drive. Third, outcome measures were based on measured net knee joint moment, which reflects the combined contribution of all muscles spanning the joint rather than that of m. quadriceps femoris alone. Taken together, the in vivo human experiments were less controlled than the in situ rat experiments described in 3  Mechanical power output during stretch–shortening cycles of rat medial gastrocnemius muscle: influence of various muscle length trajectories, which introduced several (additional) sources of uncertainty. The expectation was that this would reduce the agreement between model predictions and experimental results. Model predictions were compared with experimental data using two complementary scaling approaches. When scaling the experimental data only to the maximal isometric force of each participant, we observed good agreement between predictions and measurements (r² = 0.75). When investigating differences between conditions (expressed in units of standard deviation), the differences between model predictions and experimental data decreased, resulting in stronger agreement (r² = 0.95). These results confirmed that predictions derived with a Hill-type MTC model with generic MTC properties were valid for investigating the influence of SSC parameters on the maximally attainable AMPO of human m. quadriceps femoris in vivo. These predictions revealed that the maximally attainable AMPO peaks at a cycle frequency of about 1.6 Hz, an FTS of about 0.80, and a knee joint excursion of about 2.0 rad (centred around a knee joint angle of 1.0 rad, which is about the knee joint angle yielding maximal isometric force). Similar to rat m. gastrocnemius medialis, we found that cycle frequency and knee joint excursion strongly interacted, while the optimal FTS was again predicted to be largely unaffected by changes in cycle frequency and knee joint excursion. 4  What is the influence of knee joint movement on the maximal average mechanical power output of human quadriceps femoris muscle? provides an extensive overview of how SSC parameters influence the maximally attainable AMPO of human m. quadriceps femoris in vivo.

In 5  Limiting radial pedal forces greatly reduces maximal power output and efficiency in sprint cycling; an optimal control study, we investigated how varying constraints on radial pedal forces affect the maximally attainable AMPO during isokinetic sprint cycling. For this purpose, we used a musculoskeletal model of the human lower limb and optimised muscle activation over time to maximise AMPO summed over all muscles under different constraints on allowed radial pedal forces. Maximal AMPO decreased when lower radial pedal forces were allowed. Specifically, the maximally attainable AMPO was reduced by 53% when no radial forces were allowed compared to the condition in which there was no constraint on radial pedal forces. This reduction in maximally attainable AMPO was not only due to a decrease in positive mechanical power production by muscles, but also because some muscles substantially increased their negative mechanical power in order to satisfy the constraint on radial pedal force. Taken together, when radial pedal forces are constrained, this imposes a constraint on the resulting behaviour that must be satisfied through an appropriate choice of muscle activation over time. Consequently, muscle activation can no longer be solely optimised for the performance criterion, thereby reducing maximally attainable performance. This holds in general: requiring the resulting behaviour to satisfy additional constraints, which must be enforced through the choice of control inputs, can never improve performance; it can only leave it unchanged (if the constraint does not affect the optimal solution) or reduce it. Another example of this general principle is discussed in 6  General discussion.

In 6  General discussion, we discussed the broader implications of the findings presented in this thesis. First, we reflected on the transition from highly controlled in situ experiments on rat muscle to less controlled in vivo experiments on human muscle. Despite reduced experimental control and the use of generic rather than individualised model parameters, the Hill-type MTC models still captured the influence of SSC parameters on the maximally attainable AMPO in vivo. Second, we showed that both AMPO-optimal SSC parameters and the maximally attainable AMPO are relatively robust to variations in MTC properties. Third, we explained why no general statement can be made about the sensitivity of maximally attainable AMPO to individual SSC parameters. Fourth, we explained that the AMPO-optimal SSC-parameters of individual muscles cannot be realised in a musculoskeletal system, in which individual joints are spanned by multiple muscles. As a result, the maximally attainable AMPO summed over all muscles represents a trade-off between agonistic and antagonistic muscles. Consequently, the FTS-values that maximise summed AMPO for SSCs of individual muscles are expected to shift towards more intermediate values, i.e. closer to 0.5, especially when agonistic and antagonistic muscles have similar properties. Fifth, as explained before, in many motor tasks, motion emerges from interaction between the musculoskeletal system and the environment. Consequently, muscle activation of an individual muscle affects the MTC length over time of all muscles involved. We explained why this limits performance in the context of sprint cycling. Finally, we discussed how insights obtained in this thesis may help improve performance in motor tasks involving equipment, such as cycling and rowing. Specifically, movement tasks and equipment could potentially be designed such that the main power-producing muscles operate closer to their AMPO-optimal SSCs. Overall, 6  General discussion shows that maximising AMPO in a motor task requires consideration of both the intrinsic power-producing capacity of muscle and the constraints imposed by the task.

In short, in this thesis, we showed how SSC parameters — cycle frequency, FTS, and length excursion — influence maximally attainable AMPO. Specifically, we showed that cycle frequency and length excursion strongly interact in order to maximise AMPO, both in rat and human muscle. By contrast, the optimal FTS for maximising AMPO was relatively robust against changes in the other SSC parameters and was about 0.80–0.85 in both rat and human muscle. Since multiple muscles span the same joint, this isolated-muscle optimum cannot be realised for all muscles within a musculoskeletal system. In addition, additional constraints might further reduce the maximally attainable AMPO. The challenge for future research is therefore to determine how equipment can be designed such that the most important power-producing muscles operate as close as possible to their AMPO-optimal SSCs.
